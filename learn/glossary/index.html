Prices and charts

Ticker

The short code a stock or fund trades under. NVDA is Nvidia. A suffix tells you the exchange: .TO is the Toronto Stock Exchange, .V is the TSX Venture Exchange, and -USD marks a cryptocurrency priced in US dollars.

Closing price (the close)

The last price of the trading day, at 4 pm New York time for US stocks. Every rule on Quiplee looks only at closes, never at the price in the middle of the day, because intraday prices swing around and would trigger false signals. A weekly close is Friday's close; a month-end close is the close on the last trading day of the month.

Explained in depth: How to read a Quiplee page

Adjusted price

When a company splits its shares 2-for-1 the price halves overnight, and when it pays a dividend the price drops by that amount. Adjusted prices rewrite the history so those events don't look like real gains or losses. Quiplee uses adjusted prices, so returns include dividends.

Log scale

On a normal chart, a move from $100 to $200 looks ten times bigger than a move from $10 to $20, even though both are a 100% gain. On a log scale, equal distances mean equal percentage changes. Quiplee's growth charts use log scale so early years aren't squashed flat.

ETF (exchange-traded fund)

A fund you buy and sell like a stock, but which holds a basket of investments. SPY holds the 500 stocks in the S&P 500; GLD holds gold; TLT holds long-term US government bonds. ETFs are the usual way to own a whole market in one trade.

Index

A list of stocks used to track a market, such as the S&P 500 (500 large US companies), the Nasdaq-100, or Canada's S&P/TSX 60. You can't buy an index directly, but index funds and ETFs copy them.

Market cap

Share price multiplied by the number of shares: what the stock market says the whole company is worth. Apple is worth trillions of dollars; some micro caps on Quiplee are worth under $10 million.

Micro cap

Small public companies, usually under about $300 million in market cap (small caps run up to a few billion). Their prices swing much more than big companies, few people trade them, and the gap between buying and selling prices is wide, so trading them costs more. Quiplee charges micro caps 0.30% per switch instead of 0.05% for that reason.

52-week high and low

The highest and lowest prices over the last year. Mark Minervini's Trend Template wants a stock at least 30% above its 52-week low and within 25% of its 52-week high: well off the bottom and near the top.

Currency-hedged

A foreign stock bought in another currency moves with both the company and the exchange rate. A currency-hedged fund (like STHH, which holds STMicroelectronics) strips out the exchange-rate part so you only get the company's move.

Moving averages and trends

Moving average

The average of the last n closing prices, recalculated each day so it 'moves' along with the price. A 50-day moving average is the average of the last 50 closes. It smooths out day-to-day noise so you can see which way the price is really heading. Every rule on Quiplee is built on moving averages.

Explained in depth: Moving averages, explained

SMA (simple moving average)

Add up the last n closes and divide by n. Every day counts equally, so a big move 49 days ago matters as much as yesterday's. That makes an SMA steady but slow to react.

Explained in depth: Moving averages, explained

EMA (exponential moving average)

A moving average that weights recent prices more heavily, so it turns faster than a simple moving average of the same length. A 21-day EMA reacts to a sharp drop within days; a 21-day SMA takes longer. Faster means earlier signals but also more false ones.

Explained in depth: Moving averages, explained

Band (or cloud)

The shaded space between two moving averages, such as a 12-day and 21-day EMA. Price above the whole band is read as an uptrend, below it as a downtrend, and inside it as undecided. Band rules on Quiplee change their call only when price closes fully above or below the band, and hold their last call while price is inside it. That cuts down on false signals.

Explained in depth: Moving averages, explained

Crossover

The moment a faster moving average moves above (or below) a slower one, or price crosses an average. Crossovers are the most common trigger in trend-following rules.

Explained in depth: Moving averages, explained

Golden cross and death cross

A golden cross is when the 50-day moving average rises above the 200-day; a death cross is the reverse. They're popular in financial media because they're simple, but by the time one happens much of the move has already occurred.

Uptrend and downtrend

An uptrend is a price that keeps making higher highs and higher lows, usually sitting above a rising moving average. A downtrend is the opposite. Trend-following rules try to own stocks in uptrends and step aside in downtrends.

Support and resistance

Support is a price level (or a moving average) where declines have tended to stop and bounce. Resistance is a level where rallies have tended to stall. Traders watch both because many other traders are watching them too.

Trend following

A strategy of owning an asset while its price trend is up and stepping aside when it turns down, instead of trying to predict tops and bottoms. It accepts being late on purpose: it gives up the start and end of every move in return for avoiding most of the long declines.

Explained in depth: Trend following: why it works and when it doesn't

Momentum

The tendency for prices that have been rising to keep rising for a while, and falling ones to keep falling. Time-series momentum compares an asset with its own past, for example 'is it higher than 12 months ago?', as in the Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen rule.

Explained in depth: Trend following: why it works and when it doesn't

Bull market and bear market

A bull market is a long period of rising prices. A bear market is usually defined as a fall of 20% or more from the last peak. Trend rules earn most of their keep in bear markets, by being out of the market for much of the fall.

Stage 2 (Stage Analysis)

Stan Weinstein's model of a stock's life: Stage 1 basing (flat), Stage 2 advancing (price above a rising 30-week moving average), Stage 3 topping, and Stage 4 declining. The rule is to own stocks only in Stage 2.

Rules and signals

Trading rule (strategy)

A written-down set of instructions for when to own something and when not to, such as 'own it while the month-end price is above its 10-month average'. Because it's fixed, it can be tested on past prices and checked by anyone. Quiplee tests seven of them.

Signal

What a rule says at a given moment. On Quiplee every rule gives one of two signals: IN (hold the stock) or OUT (hold cash, earning T-bill interest).

IN and OUT (the call)

Each rule makes a call on each stock. IN means the rule holds it. OUT means the rule has sold and is sitting in Treasury bills. The call only changes when a finished close crosses the rule's line.

Explained in depth: How to read a Quiplee page

Next move (trigger level)

The exact closing price that would flip a rule from IN to OUT, or OUT to IN, at its next check. It's calculated from the rule's own formula, so if the stock closes past that level, the rule changes its call. The percentage beside it shows how far the price would have to move.

Explained in depth: How to read a Quiplee page

Next check

When the rule next looks at the price. Daily rules check every close; weekly rules check Friday's close (Sunday for crypto); monthly rules check the last close of the month. Between checks the call can't change.

Whipsaw

When a rule jumps in on a rise, then gets thrown out by a quick drop (or the reverse), losing a little each time. Sideways, choppy markets produce strings of whipsaws. Fast rules suffer them most, which is why the daily PB EMA band switches around 25 to 30 times a year on index funds.

Explained in depth: Trend following: why it works and when it doesn't

Market timing

Moving in and out of the market instead of staying invested. Every rule on Quiplee except buy-and-hold is a form of market timing. Research suggests rule-based timing mostly helps in big crashes and lags in between.

Explained in depth: Trend following: why it works and when it doesn't

Reading the results

Backtest

Running a rule on historical prices to see what it would have done. Quiplee backtests every rule on every stock from 2005 (or the stock's first trading day), with trading costs and cash interest included. A backtest shows how a rule behaved, not what it will do next.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Buy and hold

Buy it and never sell. It's the simplest strategy and the hardest to beat, so every rule on Quiplee is graded against buying and holding the same stock over exactly the same dates.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Benchmark

The yardstick a strategy is measured against. On Quiplee the benchmark is always buy-and-hold on the same stock, so a rule only 'wins' if it beats doing nothing.

Annual return (CAGR)

The steady yearly growth rate that would turn the starting amount into the ending amount, also called compound annual growth rate (CAGR). It's compounded, so 10% a year for 20 years turns $10,000 into about $67,000, not $30,000.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Total return

The full gain or loss over the whole period, dividends included. A total return of +300% means $10,000 became $40,000.

Drawdown

The drop from a peak to the lowest point before a new high. The worst drawdown is the biggest such drop in the whole test. It's the number that tells you how painful a strategy was to live with: a −55% drawdown means at the worst moment you'd lost more than half. Note it takes a +100% gain to recover from −50%.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Volatility

How much returns swing around, measured as the standard deviation of daily returns scaled to a year. The S&P 500 runs at roughly 15 to 20%; a single tech stock might be 40%; Bitcoin 60% or more. Higher volatility means a bumpier ride and bigger drawdowns.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Sharpe ratio

Return per unit of risk: the return earned above cash (T-bills), divided by volatility. It lets you compare a high-return, very bumpy strategy with a lower-return, smooth one. Higher is better. Over long periods a broad stock index usually lands around 0.4 to 0.6, and anything consistently above 1 is rare.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Risk-adjusted return

Return judged against the risk taken to earn it. Two strategies that each return 8% a year aren't equal if one fell 55% along the way and the other 20%. The Sharpe ratio is the usual measure.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Calls right (hit rate)

The share of a rule's finished calls that went the right way: an IN call is right if the price was higher by the next call, an OUT call is right if it was lower. Trend rules often score well under 50% because they take many small losses and a few large wins.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Switches per year

How often a rule changes its call in an average year. Monthly rules switch once or twice; fast daily rules can switch 30 times. Every switch costs money in trading costs and, in a taxable account, can trigger tax.

Time invested

The share of days the rule held the stock. A rule invested 75% of the time spends a quarter of its life in T-bills.

Trading costs

Commissions plus the bid-ask spread (the gap between what buyers pay and sellers get). Quiplee charges every switch 0.05% for stocks and ETFs, 0.10% for crypto and 0.30% for micro caps.

Basis point

One hundredth of one percent. 100 basis points = 1%; 5 basis points = 0.05%. Used for small numbers like trading costs and interest-rate changes.

Treasury bills (T-bills)

Short-term loans to the US government, maturing in a year or less. They're as close to risk-free as it gets, so they stand in for 'cash'. When a rule is OUT, Quiplee credits the money with the 13-week T-bill rate.

In-sample and out-of-sample

In-sample data is what a rule was designed or tuned on. Out-of-sample data is history the designer never looked at. A rule that only works in-sample was probably fitted to noise.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Overfitting

Tweaking a rule's settings until it looks great on past data. Test enough combinations and one will look brilliant by luck alone. That's why Quiplee uses each thinker's published settings instead of picking the best-looking ones.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Survivorship bias

Testing only on companies that still exist today. The ones that went bankrupt or were delisted aren't in the data, which flatters every strategy, buy-and-hold included.

Explained in depth: How to read a backtest

Using it with real money

Core and satellite

A way to split a portfolio: a large core you simply hold, plus a smaller satellite (or sleeve) where you apply a rule or take more risk. It limits the damage if the rule goes through a bad stretch.

Explained in depth: From signal to plan

Position sizing

Deciding how much money each holding gets. A common habit is capping any single stock at a small share of the portfolio, and keeping speculative names smaller still.

Explained in depth: From signal to plan

Rebalancing

Resetting a portfolio to its target mix, for example back to 70% stocks and 30% bonds after stocks have run up. It forces you to trim winners and top up laggards on a schedule.

Diversification

Spreading money across many holdings so that one company, sector or country going wrong can't sink you. An index fund is diversification in a single purchase.

Leverage

Borrowed money that magnifies results, up and down. For an investor it means buying with borrowed funds; for a company it means carrying a lot of debt. Highly leveraged companies are the ones most likely to be wiped out in a crash.

Short selling

Selling borrowed shares in the hope of buying them back cheaper. The research version of time-series momentum goes short; Quiplee only tests the long-only version an individual can run, which sits in T-bills instead.

TFSA

Canada's Tax-Free Savings Account. Investment growth and withdrawals are tax-free, within your contribution room. Because selling inside a TFSA creates no tax bill, it's the natural place to run a rule that switches in and out.

Explained in depth: From signal to plan

RRSP

Canada's Registered Retirement Savings Plan. Contributions reduce your taxable income and growth isn't taxed until you withdraw, so trades inside it don't create a tax bill along the way.

Explained in depth: From signal to plan

Capital gains

The profit when you sell an investment for more than you paid. In a regular, non-registered account, every sale is a taxable event, so a rule that switches often can create a lot of tax paperwork and bills.

Explained in depth: From signal to plan

Superficial loss rule

A Canada Revenue Agency rule: if you sell at a loss and you (or someone affiliated with you) buy the same or identical investment within 30 days before or after, the loss is denied for now and added to the cost of the new shares. Fast rules that sell and re-buy can run into it.

Explained in depth: From signal to plan

Dividend

Cash a company pays its shareholders, usually quarterly. Quiplee's returns include dividends because it uses adjusted prices.

The live desk

Revenue impact

The live desk's read on whether a news story is likely to change a company's revenue (sales). A product delay or a big new contract is revenue-impacting; a CEO interview usually isn't.

Guidance

A company's own forecast for upcoming sales or profits. When a company raises or cuts guidance, the stock often moves more than it does on the actual quarter's results.

Earnings miss

When reported profits come in below what analysts expected. The size of the stock's reaction depends on how much the miss changes the story and the forecasts.

Batting average

The desk's scorecard for a news source or analyst: of their stories that implied a direction, the share where the stock actually moved that way the next day. The model is graded the same way.

Archetype

The desk's way of grouping companies by how they tend to behave in stress, based on public facts: a fortress mega-cap with lots of cash behaves very differently in a crash from a leveraged cyclical or a micro-cap.

Crash band

The approximate range of losses that companies of the same archetype suffered in past crashes, for example −60% to −90% for leveraged cyclicals in 2008. It's a historical guide, not a prediction.

Dilution

When a company issues new shares to raise money, each existing share becomes a smaller slice of the company. Cash-burning small companies dilute often, especially after a fall.

Liquidity

How easily something can be bought or sold quickly without moving its price. Apple is very liquid. A micro cap can be illiquid: a modest order can push the price, and in a panic buyers can disappear.
