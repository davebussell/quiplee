The Hertz Lesson: Why a Crash Doesn't Care About Your Revenue

Be The Puck Editorial · September 30, 2026 · 8 min read

An 85% crash for a company that didn't sell houses

In the summer of 2007, Hertz was the biggest name in car rental — roughly $8 billion in annual revenue, an iconic brand, shares trading above $22. By January 2009 the stock closed at $3.16, down about 85%.

The crash that did it was a housing crash. Hertz didn't sell houses, didn't write mortgages, didn't hold a single CDO. So why was it one of the market's worst casualties? And the sharper version of the question: when everything was falling, why didn't money "retreat into" a big, familiar, revenue-rich company like Hertz?

The answer is hiding in the question's own wording. Hertz had revenue. It did not have liquidity — it had the opposite. Understanding that distinction is the whole game, and it's the lens for reading the next crash, whether it starts in real estate again or in AI.

Revenue is not liquidity. Liquidity is not solvency.

Three different things get blurred into "financially healthy," and a crisis tests each one separately. Revenue is what flows through the business over a year. Liquidity is what you can actually pay this month. Solvency is whether assets beat debts at honest prices.

Hertz's model made it rich in the first and desperately poor in the other two: buy hundreds of thousands of cars with borrowed money, rent them, sell them used, roll the debt forever. In 2008 that meant $1 billion of notes due in 2009 and another $5 billion in 2010, against revenue falling about 17%. Read those together: a 17% revenue decline is survivable. A closed refinancing window on $6 billion is existential. Hertz lived only by slashing costs and issuing $475 million of high-interest convertibles — emergency money at emergency prices.

The income statement is the story a company tells. The balance sheet and the maturity calendar are the facts a crisis checks.

How a crash in one sector reaches every stock

A crash is not a mood. It travels through four specific pipes, and none of them checks what sector you're in:

1 · Credit freeze2008 was a banking crash wearing a housing costume. When the lenders are impaired, every borrower is impaired — nobody can roll their debt.
2 · Demand shockHouseholds feel poorer, companies cut. Travel and construction — Hertz's two engines — stalled at once; revenue fell 17%.
3 · Forced sellingFunds facing margin calls sell what still has a bid, not what they want to sell. Even gold fell ~30% in late 2008 before recovering. Correlations go to one.
4 · RepricingRisk premiums jump for everything — and equity is a levered claim, so leverage multiplies the hit.

The fourth pipe deserves arithmetic. Take a company with a $16B enterprise value carrying $12B of debt: the stock is the $4B sliver on top. Reprice the whole business down just 20% and the debt still ranks first — the sliver absorbs the entire hit and the stock falls 80%. Hertz's −85% wasn't the market being irrational. It was the market doing exactly this arithmetic.

Where money actually hides (and why it's never a Hertz)

Frightened money doesn't rotate sideways into cheaper stocks. It moves up the capital structure and out of risk entirely: cash and T-bills first, then Treasuries (one of 2008's only rallies), then — eventually — gold, then fortress defensives like staples and utilities, which fall less. Even true fortress companies fell hard: Microsoft roughly −44% and Apple roughly −57% through 2008 (approximate), despite billions in net cash. But the crash changed their price, not their fate — both recovered within a couple of years and came out stronger.

Notice what's nowhere on that list: a leveraged cyclical. Hertz was the mirror image of a haven on every axis — cyclical demand, a balance sheet built on perpetual refinancing, a fleet of depreciating collateral. In a crisis, a Hertz isn't the shelter. It's what the shelter-seekers are running from.

Crashes reprice everyone. Leverage decides who survives the repricing.

A company that fails one check has a weakness. A company that fails four or five is a Hertz: it can post record revenue right up until the quarter the refinancing window closes.

Reading the 2026 AI cycle through the Hertz lens

Apply the scorecard to today's market and the pattern is uncomfortable, because the AI buildout is increasingly debt-financed against depreciating collateral — structurally, a fleet business. CoreWeave's GPU-backed debt has exceeded $10 billion, with Fluidstack approved for $10 billion more — loans collateralized by chips that may be economically useful for only a few years. Bank of America counts roughly $48 billion of data-center ABS and CMBS outstanding — the same plumbing that spread housing risk in 2007 — and the Bank of England has opened a probe into the sector's circular, supplier-funds-customer deal structures. Jim Chanos puts it plainly: "there's going to be debt defaults on these things."

Split the sector the way 2008 split it. Hyperscalers funding capex from operating cash flow are this cycle's Microsofts: a crash repricings them brutally but changes their price, not their fate. The leveraged capacity-builders — whose revenue depends on the boom continuing and whose debt needs rolling while it does — carry the Hertz profile. Same trap, newer collateral. None of this is a timing call: Hertz's maturity wall was visible in 2007; the window slammed in 2008. What the lens gives you is not a date. It's a watchlist.

The three questions, answered straight

Why does a crash hit companies with lots of revenue? Because crashes transmit through credit, demand, forced selling and repricing — none of which checks your sector. "Lots of revenue" is precisely what makes a leveraged company look safer than it is.

Why doesn't the market retreat into a Hertz? Frightened money buys certainty of repayment and climbs the capital structure to get it. Hertz offered the opposite — cyclical demand stacked on perpetual refinancing. It was merely large, not liquid. The market saw the difference.

What does this say about the next crash? Don't screen for revenue — screen for the Hertz profile: heavy debt, a near-term maturity wall, negative free cash flow, revenue correlated to the boom, circular funding. The casualties won't be chosen by who has customers today. They'll be chosen by who needs a lender to say yes next quarter.
